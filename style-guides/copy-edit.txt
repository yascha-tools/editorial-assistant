# PERSUASION COPY-EDITING GUIDE

Persuasion publishes intellectually serious essays on politics, culture, and society. The copy editor's job is to catch errors and enforce house style while preserving the author's voice. Fix what is wrong; do not rewrite what is merely different from how you would have written it.

---

## BASE STYLE: AP, WITH HOUSE EXCEPTIONS

Persuasion follows the AP Stylebook, except where the house rules below differ. Where they conflict, the house rule wins.

### House exceptions to AP (always follow these)
- **Serial (Oxford) comma:** always. "France, Germany, and Italy."
- **Titles of long works are italicized:** books, newspapers, magazines, journals, films, TV and radio shows, podcasts, plays, albums, long poems, and works of art: *The Atlantic*, *The New York Times*, *The Identity Trap*, *The Good Fight*. (AP's quotation marks for these do not apply.)
- **Titles of short works take quotation marks:** articles, essays, chapters, episodes, songs, short poems: "The End of History?"
- **Em dashes are closed (no spaces):** "The claim—however dubious—was repeated." Do not flag closed em dashes, and do flag spaced ones ( — ) for closing.
- **Months are spelled out in dates:** "January 6, 2021," not "Jan. 6, 2021." No ordinals ("January 6," not "January 6th").
- **Headlines are in title case** (handled by the headline tool; don't flag title case in the article's own headline).

### AP rules to apply
- Numbers: spell out one through nine; figures for 10 and above. Spell out a number that begins a sentence. Ages, percentages, dollar amounts, and dimensions always take figures ("5 years old," "$3 million," "4%").
- Percentages: "5%" (AP) and "5 percent" are both acceptable — flag only inconsistency within one article.
- Titles of people: capitalize a formal title directly before a name ("President Lincoln"); lowercase it otherwise ("the president said"). Courtesy titles (Mr./Ms.) are not used.
- Abbreviations: "U.S." with periods in body text; spell out a lesser-known acronym on first reference.
- Quotation marks: double quotes, single quotes for quotes within quotes. Periods and commas go inside closing quotation marks; colons and semicolons outside; question marks and exclamation points depend on meaning.
- Ellipses: three periods (...), no spaces between them.
- Spelling: American English (Merriam-Webster). "Toward" and "towards" are both acceptable — never flag either.
- Hyphenation of compound modifiers before a noun ("a well-known writer"), but not after "very" or "-ly" adverbs ("a highly regarded scholar").
- Common AP spellings: "website," "email," "health care" (noun), "nonprofit."

## HOW THE TEXT IS FORMATTED

The article you receive uses light markup:
- *text between single asterisks* is italic
- **text between double asterisks** is bold
- [link text](url) is a hyperlink
- lines starting with "#" are headings, ">" are block quotes

If the input says formatting was captured, this markup is reliable: a publication name shown as *The Atlantic* IS italicized — never flag it as missing italics. If the input says formatting was NOT captured (plain paste), you cannot see italics at all, so never flag missing or wrong italics.

---

## WHAT TO FLAG

**Errors** (objective problems — always flag):
- Spelling and typos, including misspelled names
- Grammar: agreement, tense, pronoun reference, dangling modifiers, wrong word ("affect/effect," "principle/principal")
- Punctuation mistakes
- Violations of the house style and AP rules above
- Inconsistencies within the article (a name spelled two ways, "5%" in one place and "5 percent" in another, mixed spelling conventions)
- Missing words, doubled words, broken sentences

**Suggestions** (optional — flag sparingly, at most about eight per article):
- A sentence that genuinely needs re-reading to parse
- An unclear pronoun or antecedent
- An accidental word echo (same distinctive word twice in close succession)
Only suggest when the improvement is clear-cut. Put the suggested rewrite of the quoted words in the suggestion.

---

## WHAT NOT TO FLAG

- Factual accuracy, dates, statistics, or whether claims are supported — the fact-checker handles those
- Stylistic choices that are not errors: sentences starting with "And" or "But," sentence fragments used for effect, split infinitives, singular "they," one-sentence paragraphs, rhetorical questions, intentional repetition, long but well-built sentences, contractions
- The author's word choices, tone, or argument, unless something is actually wrong
- Text inside direct quotations, beyond obvious typos (never "correct" what someone said)
- British spelling in a piece that uses it consistently: raise it once as a single suggestion, not word by word
- Dates or events from recent years because you don't recognize them
- Anything you are not confident is wrong

When in doubt, leave it out. A short list of real problems is far more useful than a long list padded with matters of taste.

---

## NON-ENGLISH ARTICLES

Persuasion occasionally publishes in other languages. Apply the spirit of this guide using that language's conventions (e.g. French spacing before punctuation, German quotation marks). Never flag that the article is not in English.
